# 3.1
@eq Circle: area and circumference
x: A = \pi r^{2},\qquad C = 2\pi r
w: The area of a circle equals pi times the radius squared. The circumference equals two times pi times the radius.
s: A ~ area ~ m² ; C ~ circumference ~ m ; r ~ radius ~ m
e: Body tube cross-section | A 41 mm tube has a radius of 0.0205 m: π × 0.0205². | 0.00132 m², which is 13.2 cm² {= pi*0.0205^2*1e4 ~ 13.2}
e: Bike wheel | A wheel of radius 0.34 m rolls 2π × 0.34 per turn. | 2.14 m per turn {= 2*pi*0.34 ~ 2.136}
e: End mill | A 3.175 mm bit has a cutting circumference of π × 3.175. | 9.97 mm {= pi*3.175 ~ 9.975}
c: With the drag coefficient from §1.5 | The reference area of that 41 mm rocket is its frontal circle. Drag at 50 m/s with a drag coefficient of 0.56: 0.5 × 1.2 × 50² × 0.00132 × 0.56. | 1.1 N {= 0.5*1.2*50^2*pi*0.0205^2*0.56 ~ 1.109}
u: Frontal area for drag, the area of a port or a hole, and the surface speed of a cutter (circumference × revolutions) all start here.

@eq Sphere
x: A = 4\pi r^{2},\qquad V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}
w: The surface area of a sphere equals four pi times the radius squared. Its volume equals four thirds of pi times the radius cubed.
s: A ~ surface area ~ m² ; V ~ volume ~ m³ ; r ~ radius ~ m
e: Volume of a ball-sized tank | Radius 0.11 m: 4/3 × π × 0.11³ = 0.00558 m³. | 5.6 litres {= 4/3*pi*0.11^3*1000 ~ 5.575}
e: Its skin | 4π × 0.11². | 0.152 m² {= 4*pi*0.11^2 ~ 0.15205}
c: With the square–cube law from §2.5 | Volume per unit of surface is r ÷ 3: 0.11 ÷ 3 = 0.037 m. Double the radius and it doubles, because volume grows as r³ and area as r². | 0.037 m, then 0.073 m {= 0.11/3 ~ 0.03667}
u: A sphere holds the most volume for the least wall, which is why small pressure bottles are spherical.

@eq Cylinder
x: A_{side} = 2\pi r\,\ell,\qquad V = \pi r^{2}\,\ell
w: The side area of a cylinder equals two pi times the radius times the length. Its volume equals pi times the radius squared times the length.
s: A_{side} ~ area of the curved side ~ m² ; V ~ volume ~ m³ ; r ~ radius ~ m ; \ell ~ length ~ m
e: Body tube skin | A 41 mm tube 0.6 m long: 2π × 0.0205 × 0.6. | 0.0773 m² {= 2*pi*0.0205*0.6 ~ 0.07728}
e: A bar of stock | A 20 mm aluminium rod 100 mm long: π × 0.01² × 0.1 m³. | 31.4 cm³ {= pi*0.01^2*0.1*1e6 ~ 31.416}
c: With density from §1.1 | That rod at 2700 kg/m³: 0.0000314 m³ × 2700. | 0.085 kg, or 85 g {= pi*0.01^2*0.1*2700 ~ 0.08482}
u: The mass of stock before you cut it, the skin area of a rocket body, and the volume of any tube or tank.

@eq Cone
x: A_{side} = \pi r s,\qquad V = \tfrac{1}{3}\pi r^{2} h
w: The side area of a cone equals pi times the base radius times the slant length. Its volume equals one third of pi times the radius squared times the height.
s: A_{side} ~ area of the sloping side ~ m² ; V ~ volume ~ m³ ; r ~ base radius ~ m ; h ~ height ~ m ; s ~ slant length ~ m
e: Nose cone volume | Radius 0.0205 m, height 0.12 m: 1/3 × π × 0.0205² × 0.12. | 52.8 cm³ {= 1/3*pi*0.0205^2*0.12*1e6 ~ 52.81}
e: A third of a cylinder | A cylinder with the same base and height holds π × 0.0205² × 0.12 = 158 cm³. The cone is exactly a third of it. | 52.8 cm³ {= pi*0.0205^2*0.12*1e6/3 ~ 52.81}
c: With Pythagoras, below | The slant length is √(r² + h²) = √(0.0205² + 0.12²) = 0.1217 m, so the side area is π × 0.0205 × 0.1217. | 0.00784 m² {= pi*0.0205*sqrt(0.0205^2+0.12^2) ~ 0.00784}
u: Nose-cone mass estimates, and how much stock a turned or milled nose cone starts from.

@eq Ellipse
x: A = \pi a b
w: The area of an ellipse equals pi times the semi-major axis times the semi-minor axis.
s: A ~ area ~ m² ; a ~ semi-major axis, half the long width ~ m ; b ~ semi-minor axis, half the short width ~ m
e: An elliptical pocket | Semi-axes 60 mm and 40 mm: π × 0.06 × 0.04. | 75.4 cm² {= pi*0.06*0.04*1e4 ~ 75.4}
e: A circle is the special case | With a = b = 50 mm it becomes π r². | 78.5 cm² {= pi*0.05*0.05*1e4 ~ 78.54}
c: With the circle above and sine from §3.3 | Cut a 41 mm tube at 30° to its axis and the cut face is an ellipse: b = r and a = r ÷ sin 30° = 2r. Area π × 0.041 × 0.0205. | 26.4 cm², twice the straight cut {= pi*(0.0205/sin(30*pi/180))*0.0205*1e4 ~ 26.41}
u: Closed orbits are ellipses: Chapter 4 treats them as conic sections and Chapter 29 as orbital mechanics. In the shop: elliptical fins and pockets, and any round tube cut at an angle.

@eq Pythagoras
x: c^{2} = a^{2} + b^{2}
w: In a right triangle, the square of the longest side equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides.
s: c ~ hypotenuse, the side opposite the right angle ~ m ; a, b ~ the two shorter sides ~ m
e: Three, four, five | √(3² + 4²). | 5 {= sqrt(3^2+4^2) ~ 5}
e: Squaring a frame | A 600 × 800 mm machine frame is square when each diagonal measures √(600² + 800²). | 1000 mm {= sqrt(600^2+800^2) ~ 1000}
e: A trail | 3 km on the map with 0.4 km of climb: √(9 + 0.16). | 3.03 km on foot {= sqrt(3^2+0.4^2) ~ 3.0265}
c: With square roots from Chapter 2 | A rocket drifts 120 m downwind while climbing 500 m. Its straight-line distance from the pad is √(120² + 500²). | 514 m {= sqrt(120^2+500^2) ~ 514.2}
u: Measuring both diagonals is how you square the MPCNC frame. The length of every vector in §3.5 is Pythagoras again.

@def Similar triangles
x: \frac{a_1}{a_2} = \frac{b_1}{b_2} = \frac{c_1}{c_2}
w: Triangles with the same angles have the same shape: each side of one is the same multiple of the matching side of the other.
s: a_1, b_1, c_1 ~ sides of the first triangle ~ m ; a_2, b_2, c_2 ~ matching sides of the second ~ m
e: Height of a tree | You are 1.8 m tall and cast a 2.4 m shadow. The tree's shadow is 16 m: 1.8 × 16 ÷ 2.4. | 12 m {= 1.8*16/2.4 ~ 12}
e: A tapered fin | A triangular fin is 80 mm deep at the root and comes to a point 120 mm out. Its depth 30 mm out: 80 × (120 − 30) ÷ 120. | 60 mm {= 80*(120-30)/120 ~ 60}
c: With the cone and the square–cube law (§2.5) | Slice a cone halfway up and the tip is a similar cone at half scale, so it holds 0.5³ of the volume. | 12.5% {= 0.5^3*100 ~ 12.5}
u: Scaling a drawing, reading a taper, and estimating a height from a shadow or a sighting.

# 3.2
@eq Arc length and the radian
x: s = r\,\theta
w: Arc length equals radius times angle, with the angle in radians. One radian is the angle whose arc is exactly as long as the radius.
s: s ~ arc length ~ m ; r ~ radius ~ m ; \theta ~ angle ~ rad
e: Quarter turn of a wheel | Radius 0.34 m, angle π/2: 0.34 × 1.571. | 0.53 m {= 0.34*pi/2 ~ 0.5341}
e: A full turn | An angle of 2π gives back the circumference: 2π × 0.34. | 2.14 m {= 2*pi*0.34 ~ 2.136}
e: A G2 arc | Radius 20 mm through 90°: 20 × π/2. | 31.4 mm of tool travel {= 20*pi/2 ~ 31.416}
c: With the circumference from §3.1 | An angle θ covers θ ÷ 2π of the whole circle. One radian is 1 ÷ 2π of it. | 15.9% of a turn {= 1/(2*pi)*100 ~ 15.915}
u: Tool travel on arcs, which sets cycle time in Shop Contracts; belt travel per motor turn; distance along a ground track.

@const One radian in degrees
x: 1\ \text{rad} = 57.29578^{\circ},\qquad \pi\ \text{rad} = 180^{\circ}
w: One radian is about 57.3 degrees, because pi radians make 180 degrees.
e: Degrees to radians | 30° × π ÷ 180. | 0.524 rad {= 30*pi/180 ~ 0.5236}
e: Radians to degrees | 0.1 rad × 180 ÷ π. | 5.73° {= 0.1*180/pi ~ 5.7296}
e: One motor step | A 1.8° stepper step: 1.8 × π ÷ 180. | 0.0314 rad {= 1.8*pi/180 ~ 0.031416}
c: With arc length | That step on a 16-tooth GT2 pulley, pitch radius 5.09 mm, moves the belt 5.09 × 0.0314. | 0.16 mm per full step {= 5.09*1.8*pi/180 ~ 0.1599}
u: Python's math.sin and math.cos take radians, and forgetting to convert is a classic bug. Steps per millimetre on the Jackpot3 is this arithmetic.

@eq Angular speed and tangential speed
x: \omega = \frac{d\theta}{dt},\qquad v = \omega\,r
w: Angular speed equals the rate of change of angle with time. Tangential speed equals angular speed times radius.
s: \omega ~ angular speed ~ rad/s ; \theta ~ angle ~ rad ; t ~ time ~ s ; v ~ speed of a point on the rim ~ m/s ; r ~ radius ~ m
e: Router speed | 20 000 rev/min × 2π rad per rev ÷ 60 s per min. | 2094 rad/s {= 20000*2*pi/60 ~ 2094.4}
e: Cutting-edge speed | That spindle with a 3.175 mm bit, radius 0.0015875 m: 2094 × 0.0015875. | 3.3 m/s, about 200 m/min {= 20000*2*pi/60*0.0015875 ~ 3.325}
e: Bike wheel | 10 m/s on a 0.34 m wheel: 10 ÷ 0.34. | 29.4 rad/s {= 10/0.34 ~ 29.41}
c: With the radian conversion | A rocket rolling twice per second turns at 2 × 2π = 12.6 rad/s. A fin tip 0.08 m from the axis moves sideways at 12.6 × 0.08. | 1.0 m/s {= 2*2*pi*0.08 ~ 1.0053}
u: Surface speed is the first number in every feeds-and-speeds calculation, and angular speed is what a gyro measures.

# 3.3
@def Sine, cosine and tangent on the unit circle
x: (x, y) = (\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta),\qquad \tan\theta = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}
w: A point on a circle of radius one, at angle theta from the x-axis, has x-coordinate cosine theta and y-coordinate sine theta. Tangent theta equals sine theta divided by cosine theta.
s: \theta ~ angle from the x-axis, counter-clockwise ~ rad ; \cos\theta ~ horizontal coordinate ~ none ; \sin\theta ~ vertical coordinate ~ none ; \tan\theta ~ slope of the radius ~ none
e: At 30° | cos 30° = 0.866 and sin 30° = 0.5. | the point (0.866, 0.5) {= cos(30*pi/180) ~ 0.866}
e: A trail slope | A trail climbing at 8° gains 1000 × sin 8° for every 1000 m walked. | 139 m {= 1000*sin(8*pi/180) ~ 139.17}
e: Sighting apogee | From 200 m away you sight the rocket 62° above the horizon: 200 × tan 62°. | 376 m high {= 200*tan(62*pi/180) ~ 376.1}
c: With similar triangles from §3.1 | A circle of radius r is the unit circle scaled by r, so a point on it is (r cos θ, r sin θ). A hole at 60° on a 25 mm bolt circle: x = 25 × 0.5, y = 25 × 0.866. | (12.5, 21.65) mm {= 25*sin(60*pi/180) ~ 21.651}
u: Hole positions on a bolt circle in G-code, a height from a sighting angle, and splitting any slanted quantity into components.

@eq The Pythagorean identity
x: \sin^{2}\theta + \cos^{2}\theta = 1
w: Sine squared of an angle plus cosine squared of the same angle equals one.
e: Check at 30° | 0.5² + 0.866². | 1 {= sin(30*pi/180)^2+cos(30*pi/180)^2 ~ 1}
e: Cosine from sine | If sin θ = 0.6 in the first quadrant, cos θ = √(1 − 0.36). | 0.8 {= sqrt(1-0.6^2) ~ 0.8}
c: With Pythagoras from §3.1 | It is Pythagoras on the unit circle: the two shorter sides are cos θ and sin θ, and the hypotenuse is 1. On the 25 mm bolt circle: 12.5² + 21.65² = 25². | 625 {= 12.5^2+21.651^2 ~ 625}
u: A quick test that a direction vector really has length one, and that a rotation has not stretched anything.

@eq Angle-sum formulas
x: \sin(a \pm b) = \sin a\cos b \pm \cos a\sin b,\qquad \cos(a \pm b) = \cos a\cos b \mp \sin a\sin b
w: The sine of a sum equals sine of the first times cosine of the second, plus cosine of the first times sine of the second. The cosine of a sum equals the product of the cosines minus the product of the sines. For a difference, the middle signs flip.
s: a, b ~ any two angles ~ rad
e: sin 75° from 45° and 30° | 0.7071 × 0.8660 + 0.7071 × 0.5. | 0.966 {= sin(45*pi/180)*cos(30*pi/180)+cos(45*pi/180)*sin(30*pi/180) ~ 0.9659}
e: cos 75° | 0.7071 × 0.8660 − 0.7071 × 0.5. | 0.259 {= cos(45*pi/180)*cos(30*pi/180)-sin(45*pi/180)*sin(30*pi/180) ~ 0.2588}
c: With the unit circle | Turning a point at angle a through a further angle b puts it at a + b. These formulas give its new coordinates, which is exactly what the rotation matrix of §3.6 computes. Direct check: sin 75°. | 0.966 {= sin(75*pi/180) ~ 0.9659}
u: They are why the rotation matrix works, and how two waves of the same frequency combine in signal processing.

@eq Double-angle formulas
x: \sin 2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta,\qquad \cos 2\theta = \cos^{2}\theta - \sin^{2}\theta = 1 - 2\sin^{2}\theta
w: The sine of twice an angle equals two times the sine times the cosine of the angle. The cosine of twice an angle equals cosine squared minus sine squared, which also equals one minus two times sine squared.
e: sin 60° from 30° | 2 × 0.5 × 0.866. | 0.866 {= 2*sin(30*pi/180)*cos(30*pi/180) ~ 0.866}
e: cos 60° from 30° | 1 − 2 × 0.5². | 0.5 {= 1-2*sin(30*pi/180)^2 ~ 0.5}
c: With the angle-sum formula | Put b = a in sin(a + b): sin a cos a + cos a sin a = 2 sin a cos a. At 20°: 2 × 0.342 × 0.940, against sin 40° directly. | 0.643 both ways {= 2*sin(20*pi/180)*cos(20*pi/180) ~ 0.6428}
u: Turned around, the second one says sin² averages to one half over a cycle, which is why the RMS of a sine wave is 0.707 of its peak.

@eq Small-angle approximations
x: \sin\theta \approx \theta,\qquad \tan\theta \approx \theta,\qquad \cos\theta \approx 1 - \frac{\theta^{2}}{2}
w: For a small angle measured in radians, the sine and the tangent are approximately the angle itself, and the cosine is approximately one minus half the angle squared.
s: \theta ~ a small angle ~ rad
e: Five degrees | 5° is 0.0873 rad, and sin 5° is 0.0872. | off by 0.1% {= sin(5*pi/180) ~ 0.08716}
e: Twenty degrees is less small | 0.3491 rad against sin 20° = 0.3420. | off by 2% {= (20*pi/180-sin(20*pi/180))/sin(20*pi/180)*100 ~ 2.06}
e: The cosine | 1 − 0.0873² ÷ 2, against the exact cos 5° = 0.99619. | 0.99619, the same to five figures {= 1-(5*pi/180)^2/2 ~ 0.99619}
c: With radians from §3.2 | It only works in radians. A rail that is 0.5° out of level (0.00873 rad) is off by about 600 × 0.00873 over a 600 mm span. | 5.2 mm {= 600*tan(0.5*pi/180) ~ 5.236}
u: Levelling and tramming errors in the shop, pendulums, and control near upright in Hoverslam Lab, where small tilts make the equations linear.

@def atan2: the angle in the correct quadrant
x: \theta = \operatorname{atan2}(y,\ x)
w: Theta equals the two-argument arctangent of y and x: the angle of the point (x, y) from the positive x-axis, correct in all four quadrants. Plain arcsine only returns −90° to 90°, and arccosine only 0° to 180°.
s: y ~ vertical component, given first ~ any ; x ~ horizontal component ~ same as y ; \theta ~ angle, from −π to π ~ rad
e: First quadrant | atan2(1, 1). | 45° {= atan2(1,1)*180/pi ~ 45}
e: Third quadrant | atan2(−1, −1) is −135°. Plain arctan of (−1 ÷ −1) would say 45°, the opposite direction. | −135° {= atan2(-1,-1)*180/pi ~ -135}
e: Straight up | atan2(1, 0), where y ÷ x would divide by zero. | 90° {= atan2(1,0)*180/pi ~ 90}
c: With the unit circle and Pythagoras | Wind components from a log: −3 m/s along x and +4 m/s along y. Direction atan2(4, −3), speed √(9 + 16) = 5 m/s. | 126.9° from the x-axis {= atan2(4,-3)*180/pi ~ 126.87}
u: Heading from velocity components, the angle of a toolpath segment, tilt from two accelerometer axes. Use atan2 every time; arctan(y/x) loses the quadrant.

# 3.4
@eq Law of sines
x: \frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C}
w: In any triangle, each side divided by the sine of the angle opposite it gives the same number.
s: a, b, c ~ the three sides ~ m ; A, B, C ~ the angles opposite those sides ~ rad or °
e: Find a side | Angles A = 40° and B = 60°, side a = 10 m: b = 10 × sin 60° ÷ sin 40°. | 13.5 m {= 10*sin(60*pi/180)/sin(40*pi/180) ~ 13.473}
e: Two trackers | Two people 300 m apart sight a rocket at apogee at 50° and 70° above the horizon, in line with it. The angle at the rocket is 180 − 50 − 70 = 60°. Distance from the second tracker: 300 × sin 50° ÷ sin 60°. | 265 m {= 300*sin(50*pi/180)/sin(60*pi/180) ~ 265.37}
c: With sine from §3.3 | That 265 m line of sight rises at 70°, so the height is 265.4 × sin 70°. | apogee 249 m {= 300*sin(50*pi/180)/sin(60*pi/180)*sin(70*pi/180) ~ 249.37}
u: Two-station tracking is a standard way to measure a model rocket's apogee without electronics, and a good check on an altimeter.

@eq Law of cosines
x: c^{2} = a^{2} + b^{2} - 2ab\cos C
w: The square of one side equals the sum of the squares of the other two, minus twice their product times the cosine of the angle between them.
s: a, b ~ two sides ~ m ; C ~ the angle between them ~ rad or ° ; c ~ the side opposite that angle ~ m
e: Two trails | From a junction you walk 3 km and a friend walks 4 km on trails 60° apart: √(9 + 16 − 24 × 0.5). | 3.61 km apart {= sqrt(3^2+4^2-2*3*4*cos(60*pi/180)) ~ 3.6056}
e: A right angle | With C = 90° the cosine is zero and it becomes Pythagoras. | 5 km {= sqrt(3^2+4^2-2*3*4*cos(90*pi/180)) ~ 5}
c: With Pythagoras from §3.1 | A velocity triangle: riding at 8 m/s into a 5 m/s wind that comes from 40° off the nose. The angle inside the triangle is 140°: √(64 + 25 − 80 × cos 140°). | 12.3 m/s of airspeed {= sqrt(8^2+5^2-2*8*5*cos(140*pi/180)) ~ 12.26}
u: Velocity triangles: wind plus ground speed here, and the plane-change manoeuvres of Chapter 29 later.

# 3.5
@def A vector: components and magnitude
x: \mathbf{a} = a_x\,\hat{\mathbf{i}} + a_y\,\hat{\mathbf{j}} + a_z\,\hat{\mathbf{k}},\qquad |\mathbf{a}| = \sqrt{a_x^{2} + a_y^{2} + a_z^{2}}
w: A vector is an amount with a direction. It is written as its x-part along the x unit vector, plus its y-part along y, plus its z-part along z. Its magnitude equals the square root of the sum of the squares of its parts.
s: \mathbf{a} ~ the vector ~ any ; a_x, a_y, a_z ~ its components ~ same unit ; \hat{\mathbf{i}}, \hat{\mathbf{j}}, \hat{\mathbf{k}} ~ unit vectors along x, y and z ~ none ; |\mathbf{a}| ~ magnitude, the vector's length ~ same unit
e: Wind | 3 m/s east and 4 m/s north: √(9 + 16). | 5 m/s {= sqrt(3^2+4^2) ~ 5}
e: Rocket velocity | (12, 5, 84) m/s: √(144 + 25 + 7056). | 85 m/s {= sqrt(12^2+5^2+84^2) ~ 85}
e: A tool move | G1 from (0, 0, 0) to (30, 40, −5) mm: √(900 + 1600 + 25). | 50.2 mm {= sqrt(30^2+40^2+5^2) ~ 50.249}
c: With the unit circle from §3.3 | A 20 m/s velocity at 35° above the horizon has components 20 × cos 35° and 20 × sin 35°. | (16.4, 11.5) m/s {= 20*cos(35*pi/180) ~ 16.383}
u: Position, velocity, force and wind are vectors. The three velocity columns of a flight log are one vector, and the feed of a G1 move is the magnitude of its velocity.

@eq Unit vector
x: \hat{\mathbf{a}} = \frac{\mathbf{a}}{|\mathbf{a}|}
w: The unit vector of a equals a divided by its own magnitude: the same direction, with length one.
s: \hat{\mathbf{a}} ~ unit vector along a ~ none ; \mathbf{a} ~ the vector ~ any ; |\mathbf{a}| ~ its magnitude ~ same unit
e: Wind direction | (3, 4) ÷ 5. | (0.6, 0.8) {= 3/sqrt(3^2+4^2) ~ 0.6}
e: Check its length | 0.6² + 0.8². | 1 {= 0.6^2+0.8^2 ~ 1}
c: With atan2 from §3.3 | A unit vector in the plane is (cos θ, sin θ). The vector (0.6, 0.8) points at atan2(0.8, 0.6). | 53.1° {= atan2(0.8,0.6)*180/pi ~ 53.13}
u: When only direction matters: the axis a rocket points along, or the direction of drag, which is opposite the unit vector of velocity.

@eq Dot product
x: \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = |\mathbf{a}|\,|\mathbf{b}|\cos\theta = a_x b_x + a_y b_y + a_z b_z
w: The dot product of a and b equals the magnitude of a times the magnitude of b times the cosine of the angle between them. It also equals the sum of the products of matching components. It is zero when the vectors are perpendicular.
s: \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b} ~ two vectors ~ any ; \theta ~ angle between them ~ rad ; \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} ~ the result, a plain number ~ product of the two units
e: From components | (3, 4) · (2, 1) = 3 × 2 + 4 × 1. | 10 {= 3*2+4*1 ~ 10}
e: Perpendicular | (3, 4) · (−4, 3) = −12 + 12. | 0, so they are at right angles {= 3*(-4)+4*3 ~ 0}
e: Angle between two vectors | cos θ = 10 ÷ (5 × √5) = 0.894. | 26.6° {= acos(10/(5*sqrt(5)))*180/pi ~ 26.565}
c: With magnitude | A vector dotted with itself gives its magnitude squared: (3, 4) · (3, 4) = 9 + 16. | 25, which is 5² {= 3*3+4*4 ~ 25}
u: Angle of attack is the angle between a rocket's axis and its velocity, found with a dot product. In data work the same formula is cosine similarity.

@eq Projection
x: a_{\parallel} = \mathbf{a}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{b}}
w: The part of a that lies along the direction of b equals a dotted with the unit vector of b.
s: a_{\parallel} ~ component of a along b ~ unit of a ; \mathbf{a} ~ the vector being split ~ any ; \hat{\mathbf{b}} ~ unit vector of the chosen direction ~ none
e: Headwind | Wind (−6, 2) m/s while you ride along (1, 0): −6 × 1 + 2 × 0. | −6 m/s, a 6 m/s headwind {= -6*1+2*0 ~ -6}
e: Down a slope | Gravity is 9.81 m/s² straight down. Along a 10° slope its component is 9.81 × sin 10°. | 1.70 m/s² {= 9.81*sin(10*pi/180) ~ 1.7035}
c: With the unit vector and the dot product | Wind (3, 4) m/s along the direction of (2, 1): (3 × 2 + 4 × 1) ÷ √5. | 4.47 m/s {= (3*2+4*1)/sqrt(5) ~ 4.4721}
u: Crosswind against headwind on launch day, the pull of gravity along a launch rail, and the along-track part of any measurement.

@eq Cross product
x: \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} = \left(a_y b_z - a_z b_y,\; a_z b_x - a_x b_z,\; a_x b_y - a_y b_x\right),\qquad |\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}| = |\mathbf{a}|\,|\mathbf{b}|\sin\theta
w: The cross product of a and b is a new vector at right angles to both. Its magnitude equals the magnitude of a times the magnitude of b times the sine of the angle between them, its direction follows the right-hand rule, and swapping the order flips its sign.
s: \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b} ~ two vectors ~ any ; \theta ~ angle between them ~ rad ; \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} ~ the result, a vector ~ product of the two units
e: The axes | x-hat × y-hat: the third component is 1 × 1 − 0 × 0, the others are zero. | (0, 0, 1), which is z-hat {= 1*1-0*0 ~ 1}
e: Two vectors in the plane | (3, 4, 0) × (2, 1, 0): only the z-part survives, 3 × 1 − 4 × 2. | (0, 0, −5) {= 3*1-4*2 ~ -5}
e: Swap the order | (2, 1, 0) × (3, 4, 0): 2 × 4 − 1 × 3. | (0, 0, +5), the sign flips {= 2*4-1*3 ~ 5}
c: With the dot product | The same pair are 26.565° apart (from the dot product card), so the magnitude is 5 × √5 × sin 26.565°. | 5, matching the components {= 5*sqrt(5)*sin(26.565*pi/180) ~ 5}
u: Torque and angular momentum on the next card, the normal to a surface in CAD and CAM, and the axis of any rotation.

@eq Work, torque and angular momentum
x: W = \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{d},\qquad \boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F},\qquad \mathbf{h} = \mathbf{r}\times m\mathbf{v}
w: Work equals force dotted with displacement. Torque equals the position vector crossed with the force. Angular momentum equals the position vector crossed with mass times velocity.
s: W ~ work ~ J ; \mathbf{F} ~ force ~ N ; \mathbf{d} ~ displacement ~ m ; \boldsymbol{\tau} ~ torque ~ N·m ; \mathbf{r} ~ position measured from the pivot ~ m ; \mathbf{h} ~ angular momentum ~ kg·m²/s ; m ~ mass ~ kg ; \mathbf{v} ~ velocity ~ m/s
e: Work | Pulling a sled with 50 N at 30° above the ground for 100 m: 50 × 100 × cos 30°. | 4330 J {= 50*100*cos(30*pi/180) ~ 4330.1}
e: Torque on a wrench | 40 N at right angles to a 0.2 m wrench: 0.2 × 40 × sin 90°. | 8 N·m {= 0.2*40*sin(90*pi/180) ~ 8}
e: Angular momentum | A 0.5 kg mass circling at 3 m/s on a 2 m line: 2 × 0.5 × 3. | 3 kg·m²/s {= 2*0.5*3 ~ 3}
c: With the newton and the joule from §1.1 | A gust pushes 0.4 N sideways on fins that sit 0.25 m behind the centre of mass: 0.25 × 0.4. Torque has the units of N·m but is not an energy. | 0.1 N·m, turning the nose into the wind {= 0.25*0.4 ~ 0.1}
u: Fin stability is a torque about the centre of mass, cutting torque loads a spindle, and work is the energy bookkeeping in every simulation.

@eq Gimballed thrust
x: F_{axial} = F\cos\delta,\qquad F_{lateral} = F\sin\delta
w: When the thrust is tilted by the gimbal angle delta, the part along the vehicle's axis equals thrust times cosine delta, and the sideways part that steers equals thrust times sine delta.
s: F ~ thrust ~ N ; \delta ~ gimbal angle ~ rad ; F_{axial} ~ thrust along the axis ~ N ; F_{lateral} ~ sideways, steering thrust ~ N
e: A 5° tilt | 1000 N at 5°: 1000 × cos 5° along the axis and 1000 × sin 5° sideways. | 996 N along, 87 N sideways {= 1000*sin(5*pi/180) ~ 87.16}
e: What it costs | The axial loss at 5° is 1 − cos 5°. | 0.4% {= (1-cos(5*pi/180))*100 ~ 0.3805}
c: With small angles (§3.3) and torque (above) | At 2°, which is 0.0349 rad, the sideways force is about 1000 × 0.0349 = 34.9 N. Acting 1.5 m below the centre of mass it gives 34.9 × 1.5. | 52 N·m of turning torque {= 1000*sin(2*pi/180)*1.5 ~ 52.35}
u: This is the steering in Hoverslam Lab: tilting the thrust a few degrees keeps the vehicle upright while it comes back down onto its own pad.

# 3.6
@eq Rotation in the plane
x: \begin{bmatrix} x' \\ y' \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}
w: The new x equals x times cosine theta minus y times sine theta. The new y equals x times sine theta plus y times cosine theta. Together they turn the vector counter-clockwise by theta.
s: (x, y) ~ the original vector ~ any ; (x', y') ~ the rotated vector ~ same unit ; \theta ~ rotation angle, counter-clockwise ~ rad
e: A quarter turn | (1, 0) turned 90°: x' = 0 and y' = 1 × sin 90°. | (0, 1) {= 1*sin(90*pi/180)+0*cos(90*pi/180) ~ 1}
e: Thirty degrees | (2, 1): x' = 2 × 0.866 − 1 × 0.5 and y' = 2 × 0.5 + 1 × 0.866. | (1.23, 1.87) {= 2*cos(30*pi/180)-1*sin(30*pi/180) ~ 1.2321}
e: A hole pattern | A hole at (25, 0) mm turned 60° about the origin. | (12.5, 21.65) mm {= 25*sin(60*pi/180) ~ 21.651}
c: With magnitude from §3.5 | A rotation must not change length. Before: √(2² + 1²) = 2.236. After: √(1.232² + 1.866²). | 2.236 both times {= sqrt(1.2321^2+1.8660^2) ~ 2.2361}
u: Rotating part geometry in CAM, and turning what a sensor reads in the vehicle's frame into ground coordinates.

@def Active and passive rotation
x: \begin{bmatrix} x' \\ y' \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}\quad \text{(passive)}
w: An active rotation turns the vector and leaves the axes alone. A passive rotation leaves the vector alone and turns the axes; it uses the transpose of the matrix, which is the same numbers with the two sine terms swapping signs.
s: (x, y) ~ the vector in the original axes ~ any ; (x', y') ~ the same vector described in the turned axes ~ same unit ; \theta ~ angle the axes were turned, counter-clockwise ~ rad
e: Active | The vector (1, 0) turned 30° counter-clockwise. | (0.866, 0.5) {= 1*cos(30*pi/180) ~ 0.866}
e: Passive | The same vector (1, 0), left where it is but described in axes turned 30° counter-clockwise: x' = cos 30°, y' = −sin 30°. | (0.866, −0.5) {= -1*sin(30*pi/180) ~ -0.5}
c: With the rotation matrix | Turning the axes by θ gives the same numbers as turning the vector by −θ: the y-part of (1, 0) turned by −30° is sin(−30°). | −0.5 {= sin(-30*pi/180) ~ -0.5}
u: A vehicle tilted 30° sees gravity turned the other way in its own axes. Mixing the two conventions is the most common sign error in attitude work, as Chapter 32 warns.

# 3.7
@eq Great-circle angle and distance
x: \cos d = \sin\phi_1\sin\phi_2 + \cos\phi_1\cos\phi_2\cos(\lambda_2 - \lambda_1),\qquad \text{distance} = R\,d
w: The cosine of the central angle between two points equals sine of the first latitude times sine of the second latitude, plus cosine of the first latitude times cosine of the second latitude times the cosine of the difference in longitude. The distance over the surface equals the sphere's radius times that angle in radians.
s: d ~ central angle between the two points ~ rad ; \phi_1, \phi_2 ~ the two latitudes ~ rad ; \lambda_1, \lambda_2 ~ the two longitudes ~ rad ; R ~ radius of the sphere, 6371 km for Earth ~ m
e: One degree of latitude | Same longitude, latitudes 1° apart, so d is 1° or 0.01745 rad: 6371 × 0.01745. | 111 km {= 6371*1*pi/180 ~ 111.19}
e: Along the equator | Both latitudes zero, longitudes 90° apart: cos d = 0, so d = π/2. 6371 × 1.5708. | 10 008 km, a quarter of the way round {= 6371*acos(cos(90*pi/180)) ~ 10007.5}
e: At 60° north | Two points at 60° N, 10° of longitude apart: cos d = 0.75 + 0.25 × cos 10° = 0.99620, so d = 0.0872 rad. | 555 km, half of what 10° spans at the equator {= 6371*acos(sin(60*pi/180)^2+cos(60*pi/180)^2*cos(10*pi/180)) ~ 555.4}
c: With arc length from §3.2 | It is s = rθ on a sphere: distance equals radius times angle. Ten degrees along the equator: 6371 × 10 × π ÷ 180. | 1112 km {= 6371*10*pi/180 ~ 1111.9}
u: The distance between two GPS fixes on a hike is this formula. Chapter 29 uses it for satellite ground tracks and launch azimuths.
